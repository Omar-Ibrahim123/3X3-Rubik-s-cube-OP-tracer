Blindfolded Cube Solver

The math, the Old Pochmann method, and a cube scanner

The math behind blindfolded solving

Blindfolded (BLD) solving looks like magic, but underneath it is a small amount of group theory you can hold in your head. The core idea: never think about the cube as 54 stickers — think about it as a set of pieces that need to travel back home, and solve them one swap at a time.

Pieces, not stickers

A 3×3 cube has 8 corner pieces (3 stickers each) and 12 edge pieces (2 stickers each). Centers never move relative to each other, so they define each face's "true" color. A solved cube is just every piece in its home slot, with the correct orientation (facing the right way).

Permutation and orientation

Every scramble is a permutation: it tells you which slot each piece ended up in. On top of that, each piece has an orientation — a corner can be twisted into one of 3 positions, an edge flipped into one of 2. Solving means fixing both: get each piece to its home slot and untwisted / unflipped.

Buffers — the heart of BLD

You can't move one piece without disturbing others. BLD methods get around this with a buffer: one chosen slot that acts as your "hands." You repeatedly swap whatever is sitting in the buffer with the slot where that piece actually belongs. The buffer piece shoots out to its home, and a new piece lands in the buffer — ready to be shot next.

Think of the buffer as a loading dock. A box arrives at the dock (the buffer). You read its address and send it there. Sending it away pulls the next box into the dock. Repeat until the dock is empty — the cube is solved.

This project uses the two standard Old Pochmann buffers:

  • Corner buffer: the UBL corner (Speffz letter A).
  • Edge buffer: the UR edge (Speffz letter B).

Cycles — why solving is a chain

Follow a buffer piece to its home, see what was living there, send that home, and so on. You trace a cycle: a chain of pieces that all shift along by one. When the chain loops back to the buffer, that cycle is done. Any pieces still out of place form a new cycle — you "break in" to it and keep going.

Orientation = two targets

A piece can arrive home but still be twisted or flipped. In OP that is not a special case — it just costs two targets: you shoot one of its stickers, then the other, which rotates it into place. (This is exactly why an in-place flipped edge or twisted corner shows up as a pair of letters in the trace, not one.)

Parity

Because corners and edges are solved separately, you can end up with a single leftover swap that can't be resolved within edges alone — this is parity. When it happens, one fix-up algorithm reconciles the two groups. The tracer flags it for you with [PARITY].

Lettering: turning the cube into words

To memorize a solution you convert slots into letters. This project uses the community-standard Speffz scheme: letters A–X assigned to corner stickers and, separately, to edge stickers. A solution becomes a short string of letter-pairs you can memorize like words — e.g. OP JG IT.

The Old Pochmann (OP) method

Old Pochmann is the classic "first blindfolded method." It solves the whole cube using just two algorithms plus setup moves — one swap at a time, corners and edges handled separately. It's slower than advanced methods, but it's the clearest way to understand how BLD works.

The big picture

  1. Edges first. Using the edge buffer, swap each edge home one at a time with the T-perm swap.
  2. Then corners. Using the corner buffer, swap each corner home one at a time with the Y-perm swap.
  3. Setup & undo. Before each swap you do a few "setup moves" to bring the target into the swap position, then undo them after.
  4. Parity, if needed. If edges come out odd, apply the parity fix once.

The two workhorse algorithms

Edges — T perm

R U R' U' R' F R2 U' R' U' R U R' F'

Swaps the buffer edge with the edge in the swap slot, without wrecking the rest.

Corners — Y perm

F R U' R' U' R U R' F' R U R' U' R' F R F'

The corner equivalent: swaps the buffer corner with the swap slot.

Memorizing the solution

The scanner on the next tab gives you the trace as letter-pairs. Most people turn each pair into a word or image and string them into a short story — that's how a scramble becomes something you can hold in memory behind a blindfold.

How to run the real scanner today (on your own machine)
  1. Clone the repo and install the scan dependencies: 
    pip install -r requirements-scan.txt
  2. Run a live scan with your webcam: 
    python -m app.main --scan
  3. Calibrate (show each center), spin through all six faces, confirm, and it prints the corner and edge trace.
